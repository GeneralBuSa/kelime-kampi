P: The placebo effect refers to the improvement in a patient's condition that occurs after receiving a treatment with no active ingredient, such as a sugar pill. For a long time, it was viewed mainly as a nuisance that complicated clinical trials, since researchers had to show that a new drug performed better than a placebo. Today, scientists increasingly study the effect in its own right. Brain imaging studies have shown that expecting pain relief can trigger the release of the body's natural painkillers, indicating that the effect has a measurable biological basis. The size of the effect depends on surprisingly subtle factors: injections tend to produce stronger placebo responses than pills, and expensive-looking pills often work better than cheap ones. Perhaps most surprisingly, some studies report that placebos can help even when patients are told that the pills contain no medicine. Such findings raise difficult ethical questions about whether, and how, doctors might use placebos deliberately.
